## Unit I — Comparative protist biology (target u1-protozoa / u1-nutrition / u1-proto-parasites)
### Amoeba proteus
1. Amoeba is a free-living amoebozoan commonly found in freshwater; its irregular outline changes as pseudopodia extend.
2. Locomotion involves actin-driven cytoplasmic movement and pseudopod formation; it should not be described simply as the passive flow of a liquid.
3. Food is engulfed by phagocytosis, forming food vacuoles in which digestion occurs.
4. A contractile vacuole helps eliminate excess water in freshwater conditions.
5. Asexual reproduction normally occurs by binary fission; do not generalise a particular encystment sequence to every amoeba.
### Euglena
1. Euglena is a euglenid protist; the flexible proteinaceous pellicle supports its characteristic shape changes.
2. A flagellum provides swimming; the eyespot is involved in light-direction sensing, not image formation.
3. Many Euglena species photosynthesise using chloroplasts and can use organic nutrients under suitable conditions.
4. Reproduction is commonly longitudinal binary fission.
### Volvox
1. Volvox is a colonial green alga consisting of many flagellated cells embedded in extracellular matrix.
2. Coordinated beating of flagella moves the colony; reproductive gonidia form daughter colonies.
3. In suitable species and conditions, sexual reproduction produces resistant zygotes.
4. Colonial organisation illustrates division of labour but must not be represented as the direct ancestor of animals.
### Plasmodium
1. Plasmodium species are apicomplexan parasites; infection is transmitted by an infected female Anopheles mosquito.
2. Sporozoites enter the human bloodstream and invade liver cells; merozoites subsequently invade erythrocytes.
3. Erythrocytic multiplication causes clinical disease; some parasites develop into gametocytes.
4. Sexual reproduction and sporogony occur in the mosquito, the definitive host; humans are intermediate hosts.
5. P. vivax and P. ovale can form dormant liver hypnozoites; do not attribute hypnozoites to all malaria parasites.

## Unit II — Sponge organisation and cnidarian biology (targets u2-sycon / u2-canals / u2-cnidaria / u2-obelia)
### Sponge cellular organisation
1. The pinacoderm forms much of the outer covering; choanocytes generate water currents and capture suspended particles.
2. The mesohyl contains extracellular matrix and mobile cells, including archaeocytes in many sponges.
3. Calcareous or siliceous spicules, and sometimes spongin fibres, provide structural support; skeletal composition varies among sponge lineages.
4. In the syconoid canal arrangement, water typically enters through dermal pores into incurrent canals, passes through prosopyles into choanocyte-lined radial canals, then reaches the spongocoel through apopyles and exits via the osculum.
5. Distinguish asconoid, syconoid and leuconoid canal organisation; these are structural grades, not a universal evolutionary ladder.
### Hydra and nematocysts
1. Hydra is a freshwater cnidarian polyp with an oral hypostome, tentacles, gastrovascular cavity and basal disc.
2. Its body wall contains an epidermis and gastrodermis separated by a thin mesoglea.
3. Cnidocytes contain cnidae; many tentacular cnidocytes have nematocysts used in prey capture and defence.
4. Mechanical and chemical stimuli can trigger rapid tubule discharge; avoid claiming every nematocyst injects venom.
5. Hydra commonly reproduces by budding and may reproduce sexually; regeneration depends on tissue organisation and stem-cell populations.
### Aurelia
1. Aurelia exhibits a dominant medusa phase; planula larvae settle as scyphistoma polyps.
2. Scyphistomae can undergo strobilation, releasing ephyrae that develop into medusae.
3. Compare this scyphozoan life cycle with the hydrozoan Obelia life cycle rather than treating all cnidarians as identical.

## Unit III — Flatworm and nematode comparative type studies (targets u3-platy / u3-platy-adapt / u3-nematode-parasites)
### Dugesia (planarian)
1. Dugesia is a free-living rhabditophoran flatworm with bilateral symmetry and dorsoventral flattening.
2. Its protrusible muscular pharynx leads to a branched, incomplete gut; there is no separate anus.
3. Protonephridia with flame cells contribute to excretion and water balance.
4. Neoblast stem cells underpin its remarkable regenerative capacity; regeneration varies among species and experimental conditions.
### Taenia solium
1. The adult pork tapeworm inhabits the human small intestine and attaches by a scolex bearing four suckers and an armed rostellum.
2. Its strobila comprises successive proglottids; nutrients are absorbed across the specialised tegument, and the worm lacks an alimentary canal.
3. Humans acquire intestinal taeniasis by eating inadequately cooked pork containing viable cysticerci.
4. Pigs usually acquire cysticercosis after ingesting T. solium eggs shed by infected humans.
5. Humans can also acquire cysticercosis by ingesting eggs through faecal–oral contamination; neurocysticercosis is distinct from intestinal taeniasis.
### Wuchereria bancrofti
1. Adult worms inhabit human lymphatic vessels; microfilariae circulate in blood with periodicity varying by strain and geography.
2. Mosquitoes ingest microfilariae; infective third-stage larvae develop in the mosquito and enter a human during a subsequent blood meal.
3. Lymphatic filariasis can produce lymphoedema and hydrocele; not every infection causes visible elephantiasis.
4. The vector species differs by locality; do not describe a single mosquito genus as universal.

## Unit IV — Annelid and insect type studies (targets u4-annelida / u4-modes-life / u4-arthropoda)
### Earthworm: comparative anatomy
1. Earthworms are segmented clitellate annelids. Use species-specific names and segment numbers: Pheretima and Megascolex are not interchangeable.
2. Circular and longitudinal body-wall muscles, together with chaetae and a hydrostatic skeleton, produce peristaltic locomotion.
3. Food travels through a regionally differentiated alimentary canal; crop, gizzard and intestinal arrangements vary among taxa.
4. Closed blood vessels circulate blood; respiratory gas exchange occurs predominantly across the moist body surface.
5. Segmentally arranged nephridia contribute to excretion and osmoregulation; their arrangement is species-dependent.
6. Earthworms are typically simultaneous hermaphrodites and often cross-fertilise; the clitellum produces the cocoon.
### Periplaneta americana
1. The American cockroach has a head, thorax and abdomen, with three pairs of walking legs and typically two pairs of wings.
2. Biting-chewing mouthparts include labrum, mandibles, maxillae, labium and hypopharynx.
3. Air enters through spiracles and passes through a branching tracheal system to tissues.
4. Malpighian tubules transfer nitrogenous wastes and ions from haemolymph to the gut; the hindgut conserves water and salts.
5. The open circulatory system does not principally transport oxygen, unlike vertebrate blood circulation.
6. Development is hemimetabolous: eggs hatch into nymphs that gradually develop into adults without a pupal stage.
### Insect metamorphosis
1. Ametabolous development lacks dramatic metamorphosis; hemimetabolous insects have nymphs, whereas holometabolous insects have larvae and pupae.
2. Juvenile hormone and ecdysteroids regulate moulting and developmental transitions, with timing and tissue responses depending on species.
3. Examples: cockroach—hemimetabolous; butterfly and honey bee—holometabolous.
### Apiculture and sericulture
1. Honey-bee colonies generally include a reproductive queen, female workers and seasonal male drones; caste differentiation is shaped by developmental nutrition and colony regulation.
2. Workers undertake age-related but flexible tasks including brood care, comb maintenance, guarding and foraging.
3. Silkworm Bombyx mori larvae secrete silk fibroin and sericin through silk glands; cocoons are reeled or spun depending on production method.
4. Avoid presenting all commercial honey bees or silkmoths as a single species, or equating sericulture with natural conservation of wild moths.

## Unit V — Molluscan and echinoderm diversity (targets u5-mollusca / u5-cephalopods / u5-echinodermata / u5-asterias)
### Lamellidens
1. Lamellidens is a freshwater unionid bivalve with two shell valves joined by a dorsal ligament and hinge.
2. Ctenidia support respiration and particle capture; ciliary currents help transport suspended food.
3. The muscular foot aids burrowing, and the mantle secretes the shell.
4. Reproductive and larval details differ among unionids; do not assign a universal larval host or development sequence without species-level evidence.
### Sepia
1. Sepia is a coleoid cephalopod with a mantle, paired fins, eight arms and two specialised retractile feeding tentacles.
2. The internal calcareous cuttlebone contributes to buoyancy control.
3. A muscular funnel enables jet propulsion; chromatophores and other skin structures support rapid changes in appearance.
4. The well-developed eyes and nervous system reflect advanced sensory and behavioural capacities; do not call cephalopods 'more evolved' than other molluscs.
### Sea urchins and sea cucumbers
1. Sea urchins (Echinoidea) possess a rigid test of fused ossicles and movable spines; Aristotle's lantern is present in many regular sea urchins, not all echinoderms.
2. Sea cucumbers (Holothuroidea) have elongated bodies, reduced ossicles and modified oral tube feet that act as feeding tentacles.
3. Adult echinoderms generally exhibit pentaradial organisation, whereas their larvae are bilaterally symmetrical.
4. The water vascular system operates tube feet in locomotion, attachment, feeding and other functions depending on the class.
